# Introductory Astronomy Study Notes

Astronomy describes objects and processes beyond Earth's atmosphere. A star
produces energy through nuclear fusion, while a planet shines mainly by
reflecting light from its star. The apparent brightness of an object depends
on both its intrinsic luminosity and its distance from the observer. For that
reason, a dim-looking star may be extremely powerful but very far away.

The solar system formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust. Most material
collected in the Sun, while remaining particles collided and formed planets,
moons, asteroids, and comets. The inner planets are rocky and relatively
dense. Farther from the Sun, giant planets contain large amounts of gas or
ice and have many moons and ring systems.

Earth's seasons are caused by the tilt of its rotational axis, not by a major
change in its distance from the Sun. When one hemisphere tilts toward the Sun,
it receives longer daylight and more direct sunlight. The Moon's phases occur
because we see changing portions of its sunlit half as it orbits Earth. A
lunar eclipse happens when Earth moves between the Sun and Moon.

Light provides much of astronomy's evidence. A spectrum separates light into
wavelengths, revealing chemical elements, temperature, and motion. Redshift
means light has shifted toward longer wavelengths and can indicate that an
object is moving away. Telescopes collect more light than the human eye and
allow scientists to study faint or distant targets. Radio, infrared, visible,
ultraviolet, and X-ray instruments reveal different parts of the universe.
